# Hypertrophy program design — evidence corpus

**Purpose.** This is the reference behind Dad Strength's hypertrophy programming. The users are busy fathers, mostly 30–50 years old, training 60–90 min, 3–5 days a week, often on short sleep and sometimes in a home gym. `decision-rules.md` turns this into rules. `sources.md` holds the full citations, DOIs and annotations.

**Compiled:** 2026-10-29. Literature checked against PubMed metadata and abstracts, or publisher pages for the few non-indexed items. Numbers are from abstracts unless marked *(full text, unverified)*.

## How to read this

* **Citations** are `[Author Year]`; every key resolves in `sources.md`.
* **Confidence**:
  * **Strong** — multiple meta-analyses or well-powered trials agree.
  * **Moderate** — a meta-analysis with caveats, or several consistent smaller trials.
  * **Weak** — single small trials, mechanistic or acute data, extrapolation, or expert opinion.
* **Coach practice** marks positions from practitioners (Israetel/RP, Nuckols/SBS, Helms/3DMJ, Nippard). They are heuristics, not trial evidence, and are labelled as such everywhere they appear.
* **⚠ Contested / thin** flags places where the evidence disagrees or barely exists. We say which side we lean to and why, rather than quietly picking one.

### Caveats that apply to the whole literature

* Most trials are **8–12 weeks long**, in **young adults (~20–25 y)**, often **untrained**. They usually measure size by ultrasound muscle thickness rather than MRI volume. Effects in trained 40-year-olds will be smaller and noisier. **(Strong)** — trained men gained far less than untrained men over 21 weeks [Ahtiainen 2003].
* **The individual matters more than the program.** In within-subject designs, variability between people was about 40× the variability between protocols [Damas 2019]. Only 13 of 34 people clearly benefited from tripling volume [Hammarström 2020]. **(Moderate)** Programs should therefore be defaults plus feedback loops, not fixed answers.
* **Retracted papers excluded.** Three Barbalho et al. papers that argued against higher volume or single-joint work are retracted and not used (see `sources.md` §Excluded).

## 1. Exercise selection

### 1.1 Long muscle lengths and stretch-mediated hypertrophy

**Claim: For some muscles, exercises that load the muscle hard at long lengths grow it more than exercises that load it at short lengths.** **Moderate.**

* Seated leg curl (hip flexed, hamstrings lengthened) beat prone leg curl. Whole hamstrings grew +14% vs +9%, with the difference in the biarticular heads (MRI, untrained) [Maeo 2021].
* Overhead cable triceps extension beat pushdowns. Long head +28.5% vs +19.6%, whole triceps +19.9% vs +13.9%, despite 34–39% lighter loads (MRI) [Maeo 2023]. Note that the single-joint heads also grew more, so muscle length may not be the only mechanism.
* Standing calf raise (knee straight, gastrocnemius lengthened) beat seated. Lateral gastrocnemius +12.4% vs +1.7%; the soleus grew similarly [Kinoshita 2023].
* The effect is muscle-specific, not universal. Incline curls grew biceps more and preacher curls grew brachialis and brachioradialis more, with **equal total elbow-flexor growth** [Kobayashi 2026]. A pooled meta-analysis found only a **trivial** long-length advantage for regional growth (SMD 0.05–0.09) [Varovic 2025]. Long-length benefits seem to need real external torque in the stretched position [Pedrosa 2026 review].

**Practical read:** when two exercises are otherwise equal, prefer the one that is hardest at long muscle lengths. The clearest cases are overhead triceps, seated leg curl, standing calf raise, deep squats or presses, and incline curls. It is a tiebreaker, not a mandate.

### 1.2 Lengthened partials (LPs) vs full range of motion (ROM)

**Claim: Full ROM is the default. Lengthened partials are a reasonable alternative but not superior.** **Moderate.** ⚠ Contested, though converging.

* **Evidence for LPs, mostly small trials in untrained women:**
  * LP knee extension grew quads more than shortened-range partials and matched or beat full ROM at some sites [Pedrosa 2022].
  * Calf LPs gave +15.2% vs +6.7% full ROM [Kassiano 2023a].
  * LP curls grew the distal biceps more than the short range, with no difference in summed area [Pedrosa 2023].
* **Evidence for equivalence, larger and better controlled:**
  * A pre-registered multi-site trial (n≈297, 12 wk) found LPs **not better** than full ROM for arm and thigh. Results were close, but its formal equivalence test did not pass at the pre-registered threshold, so "equivalent" is not established [Gschneidner 2025].
  * Trained lifters, upper body: no difference (Bayes factors 0.16–0.3, moderate support for the null) [Wolf 2025].
  * Trained men, MRI of the vastus lateralis: no difference [Plotkin 2026].
  * Meta-analysis: overall slightly favours full ROM (SMD 0.12). The LP-vs-full subgroup favours LPs non-significantly (−0.28, CI crossing zero) [Wolf 2023].
* **Settled:** partials in the **shortened** range are inferior [Kassiano 2023b; Pallarés 2021; Schoenfeld & Grgic 2020].
* **Outlier:** mid-range triceps partials beat full ROM by implausible margins (+48.7% vs +28.2% in 8 wk in trained men) [Goto 2019]. We do not act on it.
* **Partials after failure:** adding LPs once a set hits failure gave more calf growth (+9.6% vs +6.7%) [Larsen 2025]. That is extra volume. When volume load was equated, growth was equal (8% vs 8%), although LPs were more growth per set [Goli 2026]. **Weak.** This is an optional intensifier, not a core method.
* **Coach practice:** Nippard popularised LPs; SBS/Nuckols framed the evidence as promising but not superior. The newer trials support the cautious view.

### 1.3 Passive stretching

**Claim: Stretching alone can build some muscle, but the doses are impractical.** **Moderate.**

* Meta-analysis: hypertrophy d≈0.20, and significant only at high doses [Warneke 2024].
* About an hour a day of calf stretching matched calf training [Warneke 2023a]. 15 min/day, 4 days a week of pec stretching matched 5×12 training in an ultrasound study [Wohlann 2024].
* **Not for this population.** Time is the constraint. Loaded stretch in the lifts gets the benefit for free.

### 1.4 Free weights vs machines (stability)

**Claim: Hypertrophy is the same with free weights and machines.** **Moderate.** Only 5 trials measured hypertrophy.

* Meta-analysis of 13 studies: hypertrophy SMD −0.055 (CI −0.40 to 0.29) [Haugen 2023]. One RCT found no difference in biceps or quad thickness [Schwanbeck 2020]. The ACSM 2026 overview found equipment type did not consistently matter [Currier 2026].
* Strength gains are **modality-specific**: you get better at what you train [Haugen 2023].
* **Coach practice (RP/Israetel and others):** stable exercises (machines, cables, supported dumbbells) let you push the target muscle closer to failure with less skill, fatigue and injury risk, so they should give "more stimulus per unit fatigue." This is plausible but **untested** as a hypertrophy claim. No peer-reviewed trial isolating stability was found. ⚠
* **Practical read:** choose on access, joint comfort, ease of progression, and whether the user can take the lift close to failure safely. For a lone home-gym lifter, the last point favours machines, dumbbells and safety-arm setups over a barbell bench taken near failure.

### 1.5 Regional (non-uniform) hypertrophy

**Claim: Different exercises grow different regions of a muscle. The effects are real but modest.** **Moderate** for the existence of the effect, **weak** for any specific prescription.

* Full squats grew the glutes (+6.7% vs +2.2%) and adductors more than half squats. **Quads grew equally, and rectus femoris and hamstrings did not grow with squats** [Kubo 2019]. Deeper squats gave more front-thigh growth in another trial [Bloomquist 2013].
* Squat and hip thrust gave similar glute growth. Squats also grew quads and adductors. Glute EMG did not predict growth [Plotkin 2023].
* Leg extensions grew all regions of the rectus femoris; Smith squats did not [Zabaleta-Korta 2021].
* Foot position in calf raises shifts growth between the medial and lateral gastrocnemius [Nunes 2020].
* Compound presses grew the lateral triceps head but barely touched the long head. Overhead or lying extensions grew the long head [Brandão 2020]. Rows grew elbow flexors about half as much as curls (+5.2% vs +11.1%) [Mannarino 2021].

**Practical read:** compounds alone leave gaps in the **rectus femoris, hamstrings, triceps long head, biceps and calves**. Programs should include a knee extension, a hamstring curl or hinge, and direct arm and calf work, not compounds only. **Moderate.**

### 1.6 How many exercises per muscle, and how often to rotate

* Fixed exercises failed to grow every quad head; varied exercises grew all of them [Fonseca 2014]. A systematic review found systematic variation helps regional development, but **excessive random rotation may hinder gains** [Kassiano 2022]. Randomising exercises every session gave similar growth with better motivation [Baz-Valle 2019]. **Weak–moderate.** All studies were in young men and used ultrasound.
* **1–2 movements per muscle per week, chosen to cover different regions or lengths, is evidence-consistent.** No trial directly tests "2 exercises vs 4." That part is a design inference. **Weak.**
* **Rotation frequency:** no trial compares rotation intervals. **Coach practice** (RP, Helms, SBS) keeps exercises for at least a mesocycle (4–8 weeks) so progression can be tracked, then rotates accessories. Main lifts are often kept longer. ⚠ Thin evidence. We follow coach practice because progression tracking in an app **requires** repetition: double progression cannot work on an exercise you did once.

## 2. Load and effort measurement

### 2.1 Load and rep ranges

**Claim: Hypertrophy is similar across a wide load range (about 30–85% 1RM, about 5–30 reps) when sets end close to failure.** **Strong.**

* Meta-analyses: low (≤60% 1RM) vs high loads gave similar hypertrophy when sets went to failure [Schoenfeld 2017b]. A network meta-analysis found no hypertrophy difference between ≤8RM, 9–15RM and >15RM [Lopez 2021].
* **Limits:**
  * 20% 1RM was clearly worse: vastus lateralis +8.9% vs about +20% at 40–80% [Lasevicius 2018].
  * At 30% 1RM, sets **not** taken to failure produced little growth (+2.8% vs +7.8%). At 80%, failure did not matter [Lasevicius 2022].
  * **Light loads require high effort. Heavy loads tolerate a little slack.**
* **Strength** favours heavy loads [Schoenfeld 2017b; Lopez 2021; Schoenfeld 2015].
* **Practical consideration:** very high-rep sets are aerobically brutal and time-consuming, and **RIR estimates are least accurate above 12 reps** (§2.3). Very heavy sets (<5 reps) carry more joint load per set. These are why most practitioners use **6–15 reps** as the default range, with 5–8 on compounds and 10–20 on isolations. **Coach practice**, consistent with the evidence.

### 2.2 Proximity to failure (how hard each set should be)

**Claim: Sets should end around 0–3 reps in reserve (RIR). Going all the way to failure adds little hypertrophy and more fatigue.** **Moderate**, converging.

* Meta-regression across studies: hypertrophy **improves as sets end closer to failure**; strength is largely unaffected by RIR [Robinson 2024]. RIR in included studies was *estimated*, and the slope magnitude is in the full text *(unverified)*.
* Meta-analysis: failure vs non-failure gave a **trivial** advantage (ES 0.19) under any definition. True momentary failure vs non-failure showed no significant advantage (ES 0.12, CI crossing 0) [Refalo 2023]. An earlier meta-analysis showed no difference when volume was equated [Vieira 2021]. A third found a small advantage only in trained subjects (ES 0.15) [Grgic 2022].
* Direct trials in trained lifters:
  * Failure vs 1–2 RIR gave **identical** quad growth (0.181 vs 0.182 cm) [Refalo 2024b].
  * Elbow flexors at 1–3 RIR vs failure: no difference [Vasconcelos 2026].
  * One set per exercise to failure vs with RIR slightly favoured failure [Hermann 2025].
* **Cost of failure:** more discomfort and RPE, worse affect [Refalo 2025], and more velocity and rep loss on later sets [Refalo 2024b].
* **Velocity-loss studies agree:** higher velocity loss within a set (closer to failure) adds slightly more hypertrophy [Jukic 2023; Pareja-Blanco 2017, 2020; Hickmott 2022].
* ⚠ **The contested part is the shape near zero.** One reading of Robinson is "closer is always better." The direct trials say 0 vs 1–2 RIR is flat. **Our reading:** 0–3 RIR is near-optimal and ≥4–5 RIR leaves growth on the table. Reserve 0 RIR for the last set of safe isolation exercises.
* **Coach practice:** RP ramps RIR from about 3 down to 0–1 across a mesocycle, then deloads. Helms/3DMJ uses mostly 1–3 RIR with occasional failure on isolations. SBS/Nuckols recommends mostly 0–3 RIR.

### 2.3 How accurately people judge RIR

This decides what an app can trust.

| Finding | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| On average, people underpredict reps to failure by about **1 rep** (pooled mean bias 0.95, CI 0.17–1.73). That is the group average, not how far off any one person is; heterogeneity is huge (I²≈98%) | [Halperin 2022] | Moderate |
| Accuracy **improves as the set nears failure** | [Halperin 2022; Remmert 2023a; Hackett 2012] | Moderate–strong |
| Accuracy is **much worse in sets of more than 12 reps** (β 0.47 vs 0.06) | [Halperin 2022; Remmert 2023b] | Moderate |
| Accuracy is better in **later sets** than in set 1 | [Halperin 2022; Hackett 2022] | Moderate |
| **Training experience has little or no effect** (meta-regression β≈0) | [Halperin 2022; Remmert 2023a; Refalo 2024a] | Moderate |
| Trained lifters on bench at 75% 1RM: absolute error **0.65 reps**; 1- and 3-RIR calls equally accurate | [Refalo 2024a] | Moderate |
| Some studies show larger errors: about 2 reps (knee extension, trained) and about 3-rep SEM (mixed) | [Armes 2020; Steele 2017] | Weak–moderate |
| Six weeks of practice did **not** improve accuracy | [Remmert 2023b] | Weak (n=9) |
| Experienced lifters rate true max attempts closer to RPE 10 | [Zourdos 2016; Ormsbee 2019] | Moderate |

⚠ **Experience level:** the popular belief that novices cannot use RIR is **only weakly supported**. The pooled data show error is driven by **rep count and distance from failure**, not years of training. Novices are bad at it in the same ways as everyone else. Their real problem is likely having never been near failure, so they may call sets easier than they are (coach observation, not a measured figure).

**Coach practice:** have users occasionally take a safe set to failure (an AMRAP on a machine or isolation) to **calibrate** their RIR sense. This is consistent with accuracy improving near failure, but no trial tests calibration as an intervention. **Weak.**

### 2.4 The methods compared

| Method | Hypertrophy outcome vs alternatives | Accuracy / precision | Ease for novices | App fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **%1RM** | Similar to RPE-based loading [Helms 2018; Hickmott 2022] | Needs a valid 1RM. Reps at a given % vary widely between people and lifts. Fixed % often accidentally drives sets to failure [Cowley 2025] | Poor: 1RM testing is risky and noisy for novices, and meaningless for isolation work | Good only for main barbell lifts with a known max |
| **Fixed rep range ("3×8–12")** | Fine **if** sets are taken near failure (§2.1) | No effort control on its own. People stop at 12 whatever the RIR | Very easy | Needs an effort anchor added |
| **RPE (1–10)** | Similar to %1RM. Possible small strength edge [Helms 2018] | Same as RIR, since the scale is defined by RIR | Moderate: "RPE 8" is abstract | Fine for experienced users |
| **RIR ("stop with 2 left")** | As RPE | About 1 rep mean error; worse at >12 reps and in early sets (§2.3) | **Easiest effort concept to explain**: "how many more could you have done?" | **Best default** |
| **Velocity (VBT)** | Velocity-loss thresholds work. More loss means slightly more hypertrophy [Jukic 2023] | Most objective; beat subjective RIR in early sets and at lighter loads [Hickmott 2024; Cowley 2025] | Needs a device. Consumer sensors are valid only in narrow ranges [Orange 2019]; load-velocity 1RM estimates are too noisy to prescribe from [Hughes 2019] | **Not viable as a default.** Optional later for owners of a validated device |
| **Autoregulation generally** | Matches fixed loading for hypertrophy and strength [Hickmott 2022; Larsen 2021]. Greig 2020 is a terminology review, not outcome data | — | — | Use as a weekly adjustment layer |

### 2.5 Recommendation for the app (opinionated)

**Use rep range + RIR target + double progression. Make reps the objective signal and RIR the safety and effort rail.** Keep %1RM only for main barbell lifts where a max exists. Do not use VBT by default.

Why:

1. **RIR is the most explainable effort anchor** and costs nothing (§2.4).
2. RIR error is about 1 rep and biased toward underestimating capacity. Pair it with something the app can measure: **reps completed at a given load**. If a user logs 2+ reps above the top of the range at the prescribed RIR, the load is too light, whatever they say about RIR. Reps are ground truth; RIR is a noisy self-report.
3. Prescribe **1–3 RIR**, not 0, on compounds. Given the error, a "1 RIR" call is often really 2–3 [Halperin 2022], which is still in the productive zone (§2.2). Asking for "0 RIR" on a barbell squat is unsafe for a lone lifter. Allow 0 RIR on the last set of isolation and machine work, where failure is safe and it calibrates the user.
4. Keep isolation rep ranges **at or below about 15–20**, or treat high-rep sets as "to failure / last set AMRAP," because RIR estimates fall apart above 12 reps.
5. Show **RPE as a translation, not a separate system** (RPE = 10 − RIR [Zourdos 2016]). The current engine stores `targetRpe` for %-based work and `targetRir` for range work. That is consistent with this.

## 3. Volume

### 3.1 Dose-response

**Claim: More weekly hard sets per muscle give more hypertrophy, with diminishing returns. The curve does not clearly plateau inside the studied range.** **Strong** (direction), **moderate** (shape).

* Meta-regression of 67 studies (n=2,058): the probability of a positive volume slope for hypertrophy was about 100%, with **diminishing returns** (more pronounced for strength) [Pelland 2026].
* Each added weekly set ≈ +0.37% gain. Higher vs lower volume ≈ +3.9 percentage points. The <5 / 5–9 / 10+ sets categories showed a trend [Schoenfeld 2017a]. That analysis was mostly untrained subjects, with few groups above 10 sets.
* The ACSM 2026 position stand (overview of 137 systematic reviews) lists **≥10 sets/muscle/week** as enhancing hypertrophy [Currier 2026].
* Minimum dose: one set of each of 9 exercises, twice a week, still produced appreciable gains in trained people [Hermann 2025]. 13-minute sessions three times a week matched higher volumes for **strength**, though not for size [Schoenfeld 2019].

### 3.2 The contested upper end (>~20 sets/muscle/week)

⚠ **Contested.** The table gives both sides.

| Pro "more is better" | Null or plateau |
|---|---|
| 5 vs 3 vs 1 sets per exercise: highest growth at about 45 quad sets/week [Schoenfeld 2019] (set count from full text) | 12/18/24 lower-body sets in strong trainees: no difference; 12 sets was numerically best [Aube 2022] |
| 32 > 16 sets for leg and triceps thickness [Brigatto 2022] (n=9/group) | 9/18/27 biceps sets: an inverted U, not significant [Heaselgrave 2019] |
| Adding 4 sets every 2 weeks from 22 increased vastus lateralis cross-sectional area in women [Enes 2025]; in men the trend was non-significant [Enes 2024] | 3/6/12 sets: no difference [Ostrowski 1997] |
| Indirect-set-weighted fractional model still slopes up [Pelland 2026] | Extreme volume (up to 32 sets/exercise/week): gains mostly in weeks 0–3, dampened above about 20. Fibre growth was partly sarcoplasmic, with actin and myosin concentration down about 30% [Haun 2018, 2019] |

**Our reading:**

* For this population, **10–20 fractional sets/muscle/week** is the productive band. Above 20 is not proven useless, but the evidence is mixed. Those studies used young lifters training under supervision with no jobs or kids, and the swelling-vs-muscle question is open.
* The real constraint here is **time and recovery**, not the curve.
* Baz-Valle 2022 (moderate 12–20 vs high >20: no difference for quads or biceps; high better for triceps) supports 12–20 as a sensible standard. **Moderate.**

### 3.3 Per-session volume

⚠ **Thin.** The only quantitative estimate is a **non-peer-reviewed preprint**. Per-session hypertrophy benefit kept rising up to about **11 fractional sets per muscle per session**, beyond which additional benefit was undetectable. The data at high per-session volumes are sparse [Remmert 2025 preprint]. Splitting the same sets across two sessions sped recovery acutely [Bartolomei 2023].

**Coach practice:** about 6–10 hard sets per muscle per session as a working ceiling. We adopt **≤10 direct sets per muscle per session** and treat it as weak evidence.

### 3.4 Counting indirect sets

**Claim: Count a set as 1 for the muscles it trains directly and 0.5 for muscles it trains meaningfully but indirectly.** **Moderate.**

* Fractional counting (indirect = 0.5) fit the data better than counting indirect sets as 1 or as 0 [Pelland 2026].
* Mechanistic support:
  * Bench press grew the lateral triceps head but **not the long head**, and did not significantly grow total triceps cross-sectional area [Brandão 2020].
  * Rows grew elbow flexors about half as much as curls [Mannarino 2021].
  * Older "compounds are enough for arms" trials used circumference or untrained people and found no difference [Gentil 2013; de França 2015]. Weak, and some of that author group's other work is retracted.
* **Mapping (design inference, weak):**

| Exercise | Direct (1.0) | Indirect (0.5) |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal press | chest | front delts, triceps |
| Vertical press | front delts | triceps, side delts |
| Row | upper back, lats | biceps, rear delts |
| Pull-up / pulldown | lats | biceps, upper back |
| Squat / leg press | quads | glutes, adductors |
| Hinge (RDL) | hamstrings, glutes | spinal erectors, adductors |

* **Not credited:** the triceps long head from presses, rectus femoris from squats [Kubo 2019], and hamstrings from squats [Kubo 2019].

### 3.5 Volume landmarks: MEV / MAV / MRV (coach practice)

**Renaissance Periodization (Israetel) definitions** [RP 2017; RP chest guide 2024]:

* **MV**, maintenance volume: about 6 sets/week when trained at least 2×/week.
* **MEV**, minimum effective volume: the lowest volume that grows muscle.
* **MAV**, maximum adaptive volume: the range of best gains.
* **MRV**, maximum recoverable volume: the most you can recover from.

Example, chest for intermediates (sets/week): MV 2–4, MEV 4–6, MAV 6–16, MRV 16–24. These ranges differ by muscle; traps, for example, list MEV 0–4 and MRV 12–20.

RP progresses a mesocycle by adding sets weekly from near MEV toward MRV. The weekly adjustment uses soreness, pump and performance scores. After that comes a deload at about MV.

**Status: unvalidated heuristics.**

* No trial has measured individual MEV or MRV.
* Indirect support:
  * Maintenance needs far less volume than growth: 1/3 to 1/9 of the training dose preserved hypertrophy in young adults [Bickel 2011].
  * Individual volume needs vary [Hammarström 2020; Damas 2019].
  * Increasing from each person's **own recent volume** helps: 1.2× logged volume beat a standard 22 sets [Scarpelli 2022], with a retrospective correlation between volume increase and growth [de Camargo 2023].

**Useful for the app as a vocabulary and a progression frame**, not as validated numbers.

## 4. Frequency and splits

### 4.1 Frequency

**Claim: When weekly volume is equal, training a muscle 1× vs 2–3× per week makes little difference to hypertrophy. Frequency is a tool for fitting volume in and spreading fatigue.** **Strong.**

* Volume-equated frequency made no significant difference, including in trained subjects [Schoenfeld 2019b; Pelland 2026, where the frequency effect was compatible with negligible]. An older meta-analysis favoured 2×/week [Schoenfeld 2016]; it is superseded by the volume-equated analysis.
* Specific trials:
  * 3× vs 6×/week: similar [Saric 2019; Colquhoun 2018].
  * 1× vs 2×/week at 16 sets: similar, with effect sizes slightly favouring 2× [Brigatto 2019].
* Strength does benefit modestly from higher frequency [Grgic 2018; Pelland 2026], because each session is skill practice.

### 4.2 Split vs full body

**Claim: No inherent hypertrophy difference when volume is equal.** **Moderate–strong.**

* Meta-analysis of 14 studies: split vs full body showed no hypertrophy or strength difference when volume-equated [Ramos-Campo 2024].
* Individual trials conflict:
  * Split better for quads [Bartolomei 2021].
  * 5× full body better than a 1×/week split [Zaroni 2019].
* ⚠ Single trials point both ways; trust the meta-analysis.

**So the split choice is about logistics, session length, per-session volume caps, recovery spacing, and robustness to missed sessions**, not about growth per se.

### 4.3 Split options for 3, 4 and 5 days

The evidence says these are all roughly equivalent at equal volume. The fit judgements below are **design reasoning** from §3.3 (per-session cap), §4.1 and the dad constraints.

| Days | Split | Frequency per muscle | Fits | Watch-outs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | **Full body (A/B/C)** | 3× | Default for 3 days, beginners, and anyone likely to miss sessions: a missed day costs about 1/3 of the week, not a whole muscle | Sessions get long if every muscle gets 3–4 sets. Keep about 2–3 exercises per region and rotate emphasis |
| 3 | Upper/Lower/Full | 1.5–2× | Intermediate on 3 days who wants more per-session focus | Uneven frequency |
| 3 | PPL (1× each) | 1× | Only someone who loves it | 1× means ~10–20 sets in one session, past the ~10/session ceiling, and a missed day loses a whole muscle group for a week. **Not recommended** |
| 4 | **Upper/Lower ×2** | 2× | Default for 4 days. Best fit for 60–90 min sessions. Evidence-consistent and simple | Upper days are crowded. Use supersets |
| 4 | Full body ×4 | 4× | Short sessions (≤60 min) | More warm-up overhead in total |
| 5 | **Upper/Lower/Push/Pull/Legs (hybrid)** | ~2× | Default for 5 days. Intermediate+ with time | Recovery demand. Drops cleanly to U/L if a day is lost |
| 5 | U/L/U/L + arms/delts/weak-point day | 2–3× | Specialisation phases | — |
| 5–6 | PPL ×2 | 2× | Rarely realistic for dads | 6 days |
| any | "Bro split" (one muscle per day) | 1× | Enthusiasts who want it for enjoyment | Hypertrophy is fine at equal volume [Schoenfeld 2019b], but it breaks the per-session cap and is fragile to missed days. **Not a default** |

**Coach practice agreement:** RP, SBS and Helms all default to training each muscle about 2×/week, mainly to distribute volume. This matches the evidence framing.

## 5. Progression and periodization

### 5.1 Progressive overload: load vs reps

**Claim: Adding reps at a fixed load and adding load at fixed reps produce similar hypertrophy.** **Moderate** (one good trial).

* Trained lifters, 8 wk: load progression and rep progression gave similar muscle thickness; strength slightly favoured load progression [Plotkin 2022].
* This is the evidential basis for **double progression**: hold the load until all sets hit the top of the rep range at the target RIR, then add the smallest load step and drop back to the bottom of the range. That is exactly what `repRange` + `loadStepLbs` implements.

### 5.2 Periodization models

**Claim: Periodization reliably helps strength but has not been shown to help hypertrophy. Linear and undulating models are equal for hypertrophy.** **Strong.**

* Periodized vs non-periodized:
  * 1RM ES 0.31 (significant); **hypertrophy ES 0.13 (n.s.)** [Moesgaard 2022].
  * Periodized beat non-periodized for 1RM in an earlier meta-analysis [Williams 2017].
* Linear vs undulating:
  * Hypertrophy: d −0.02, no difference [Grgic 2017].
  * Hypertrophy, again no difference; strength favours undulating in trained lifters (ES 0.61) [Moesgaard 2022].
  * Strength: no difference [Harries 2015].
  * A narrative review concludes periodization's necessity for hypertrophy is unclear [Evans 2019].
* The ACSM 2026 overview: periodization does not consistently enhance outcomes [Currier 2026].
* **Block periodization** for hypertrophy: no direct hypertrophy trials found. ⚠ Thin. Block models come from strength sport.

**Practical read:** for hypertrophy, "periodization" means **managing fatigue and progression over time**, not elaborate phase models:

* mesocycles of accumulating effort or volume, then a deload
* exercise rotation between mesocycles
* optional heavier and lighter days, which are fine and add variety, but are not needed for growth

### 5.3 Mesocycle length

⚠ **No trial compares mesocycle lengths.** It is all **coach practice**:

* RP uses about 4–6 weeks of accumulation plus a deload.
* Coaches report deloading every 4–6 weeks [Bell 2022].
* Athletes report deloading every **5.6 ± 2.3 weeks**, lasting 6.4 ± 1.7 days [Rogerson 2024].

We adopt **4–6 week mesocycles (default 5 = 4 hard weeks + 1 deload)** as convention, not evidence.

### 5.4 Deloads

**Claim: Short planned breaks do not cost hypertrophy. There is no evidence that deloads enhance it.** **Weak–moderate.** ⚠ Thin.

* A one-week **complete break** mid-program in trained lifters cost no hypertrophy but slightly reduced lower-body strength gains [Coleman 2024].
* Reduced-volume deload weeks (down to one session of 2 sets in weeks 4 and 8) cost nothing in untrained young men [Pancar 2026].
* 6 weeks on / 3 weeks off ended equal to continuous training after 24 weeks [Ogasawara 2013]. Maintenance at 1/3 to 1/9 of the dose preserved size [Bickel 2011].
* Delphi consensus definition: "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness" [Bell 2023]. This is expert opinion.
* **How deloads are done in practice:**
  * Athletes cut volume, load and effort but keep frequency and exercise selection [Rogerson 2024].
  * Coaches commonly cut volume 0–25%, with wide variance [De Marco 2024].
  * Interviewed physique coaches cut volume and effort [Bell 2022].
* **Practical read:** for busy dads the deload's value is mostly **joint and psychological recovery and adherence**, and it is cheap insurance. Life frequently imposes unplanned deloads (sick kids, travel). Treat those as the deload and reset the mesocycle clock rather than stacking a planned one on top. **Coach practice / design inference.**

## 6. Training style

### 6.1 Failure vs RIR

See §2.2. Summary: 0–3 RIR is near-optimal, true failure adds fatigue for little gain, and very far from failure is worse. **Moderate.**

### 6.2 Rest periods

**Claim: Rest of at least 60–90 s gives a small hypertrophy advantage over shorter rest. Beyond about 90–120 s there is little further benefit, unless longer rest is needed to keep performance up.** **Moderate.**

* Bayesian meta-analysis: a small benefit from resting more than 60 s, and no apparent difference beyond about 90 s [Singer 2024].
* Trained men: 3 min beat 1 min [Schoenfeld 2016b], but volume load was not equated.
* **Mechanism: rest matters because it preserves volume load.** With volume load matched, rest made no difference [Longo 2022]. With low loads to failure, 30 s and 150 s were similar [Fink 2017].
* **Practical read:**
  * Compounds about 2–3 min. Isolation about 60–90 s.
  * Short rest is acceptable when total reps and sets are maintained, for example by adding a set or using paired sets.

### 6.3 Tempo

**Claim: Any rep duration from about 0.5 to 8 s works. Avoid deliberately super-slow reps (>10 s).** **Moderate** (few studies).

* Rep durations of 0.5–8 s gave similar hypertrophy [Schoenfeld 2015b]. Phase-specific (eccentric) recommendations are not evidence-based [Wilk 2021].
* Slower tempos reduce usable load and reps [Wilk 2020].
* A narrative review suggests about 2–8 s reps with control in the stretch [Androulakis Korakakis 2023].
* **For dads:** controlled lowering, no bouncing, and no prescribed slow tempos. Slow tempos cost time for nothing.

### 6.4 Time savers: supersets, drop sets, rest-pause/myo-reps, cluster sets

**Claim: These match traditional sets for hypertrophy and save time. The cost is higher perceived effort and short-term fatigue.** **Moderate** for equivalence; **weak** for any advantage.

| Technique | Hypertrophy vs traditional sets | Time | Cost | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Antagonist supersets** (e.g. row + press) | Equal (SMD −0.05) | Much more efficient (SMD 1.74). Volume load **maintained or higher** | Higher RPE and lactate | [Zhang 2025; Robbins 2010a; Paz 2017] |
| Same-muscle supersets | — | Faster | **Volume load drops** (SMD −1.08) | [Zhang 2025] |
| Upper/lower or tri-sets | — | More efficient | 24-h CK and jump impairment higher | [Weakley 2017] |
| **Drop sets** | Equal (SMD 0.155 n.s.; ES 0.08; SMD 0.04) | Half to one-third of the time in some protocols | RPE much higher (SMD 1.62) | [Sødal 2023; Coleman 2022; Havers 2026; Fink 2018] |
| **Rest-pause / myo-reps** | Equal [Enes 2021]; one small trial favoured it [Prestes 2019]; modest advantage in a 2026 meta-analysis | Faster | Higher effort | [Enes 2021; Prestes 2019; Tsartsapakis 2026] |
| Cluster sets | Equal (ES −0.05) | **Slower**, saves fatigue not time | — | [Davies 2021] |

* Review consensus: supersets, drop sets and rest-pause can roughly halve session time while keeping volume [Iversen 2021]. Evidence is too thin for dose guidelines [Krzysztofik 2019].
* **Ranking for this app** (design inference):
  1. Antagonist or non-competing supersets: biggest time saving, no volume loss.
  2. Myo-reps or rest-pause on isolations.
  3. A drop set on the last set of an isolation.
* Avoid same-muscle supersets and intensifiers on heavy compounds.

### 6.5 Cardio interference

**Claim: Moderate cardio does not meaningfully impair whole-muscle hypertrophy. It can blunt lower-body strength and power, mainly in trained lifters and when done in the same session.** **Moderate.** ⚠ Contested on details.

* Latest meta-analysis of 43 studies: **no hypertrophy penalty** (SMD −0.01), no 1RM penalty, but explosive strength reduced, more so in same-session training [Schumann 2022]. No moderation by running vs cycling, ≥5 vs <5 sessions/week, training status, or age above vs below 40.
* Fibre-level analysis: a small penalty (SMD −0.23), with Type I fibres blunted by running but not cycling [Lundberg 2022].
* Older meta-analysis: hypertrophy ES 1.23 for strength training alone vs 0.85 for concurrent. Running, not cycling, hurt. Longer and more frequent cardio was worse [Wilson 2012].
* Lower-body 1RM is blunted in **trained** people, mainly in same-session training [Petré 2021]. Lifting before cardio in a session is better for leg strength [Murlasits 2018].
* HIIT: no hypertrophy penalty, a small lower-body strength penalty. ⚠ **Cycling HIIT trended worse than running HIIT**, which contradicts the "cycle, don't run" rule [Sabag 2018].
* **Our reading:** for dads, cardio is a **health priority**, and the hypertrophy cost at sensible doses is about zero. Rules of thumb:
  * Lift first if in the same session.
  * Separate hard leg sessions from hard intervals when possible.
  * Keep volume moderate.
* "6+ hours apart" and "cycling over running" are **coach practice**, not established thresholds. Schumann found differences only between same-session and ≥3 h apart.

## 7. Special cases

### 7.1 Lifters aged 35–50

⚠ **Major evidence gap.** Almost all "ageing" data are from people over 50 or 60.

* What exists for this age band:
  * Responses were not affected by age across 19–78 y (pooled, untrained) [Ahtiainen 2016].
  * Men averaging 47 y recovered from damaging exercise **the same as** 22-year-olds over 48 h [Gordon 2017].
  * Older athletes *feel* slower to recover, but most studies confound age with activity level [Fell 2008].
* Over 50, growth still happens but is smaller with age (β −0.03/yr), and higher volume helps (β 0.05) [Peterson 2011]. Anabolic resistance appears in the elderly [Breen 2011]. The NSCA position stand covers older adults [Fragala 2019]. Recovery from muscle-damaging exercise is slower after 50 [Clifford 2019].
* **Protein:** the benefit of supplementation plateaued at about **1.6 g/kg/day**, with the effect slightly smaller with age [Morton 2018]. **Strong** for 1.6 g/kg as a target.
* **Our reading:** program 35–50-year-olds **like younger adults**, with individualised recovery. **Moderate.**
* **Coach practice** (no age-specific trials):
  * longer warm-ups
  * joint-friendly exercise choices (dumbbells and machines over barbell for painful joints, neutral grips, avoiding very low reps on cranky joints)
  * slightly more conservative weekly volume increases
  * readiness to deload reactively
* Training status matters more than age. Detrained returning fathers will gain fast regardless [Ahtiainen 2003].

### 7.2 Limited time

**Claim: Big gains come from a small, well-structured weekly dose. Time is best saved by structure, not by cutting sets that matter.** **Moderate.**

* Recommendations from a time-efficiency review [Iversen 2021]:
  * prioritise bilateral compounds
  * at least 4 sets/muscle/week at 6–15RM
  * weekly volume matters more than frequency
  * supersets, drop sets and rest-pause about halve session time
  * keep warm-ups specific and skip stretching unless flexibility is a goal
* Minimal doses:
  * 1 set per lift 2–3×/week to near failure builds strength [Androulakis-Korakakis 2020].
  * Low-dose "exercise snacks" improve strength and function [Fyfe 2022].
  * 3×13-minute sessions matched higher volume for strength, though less for size [Schoenfeld 2019].
* **Floors** for the app:
  * At least 4 sets/muscle/week keeps progress going [Iversen 2021].
  * About MV (≈1/3 of the usual dose) maintains muscle [Bickel 2011].
* **Time arithmetic (design estimate, not measured):** a working set plus rest takes about 2.5–3 min straight, or about 1.5–2 min per set when supersetted.

| Session length | Warm-up | Working sets, straight sets | Working sets, mostly supersetted |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 min | ~10 min | ~16–20 | ~25 |
| 90 min | ~10 min | ~26–30 | ~35+ |

Per-session muscle caps (§3.3) usually bind before these totals do.

### 7.3 Poor sleep

⚠ **Mechanistic and acute evidence only. No long-term trial shows sleep loss reduces hypertrophy in lifters.**

* **Acute performance loss** (moderate–strong):
  * Acute sleep loss reduced performance about 7.6% overall, including strength. Performance fell about 0.4% per hour awake before exercise. **Morning sessions were largely spared; evening sessions were hurt** [Craven 2022].
  * Consecutive restricted nights reduce multi-joint strength [Knowles 2018; Reilly 1994].
  * Restriction cut bar velocity up to 15% and raised session RPE 11% with little volume-load loss [Knowles 2022].
* **Mechanistic (weak for hypertrophy outcomes):**
  * One night of total deprivation cut muscle protein synthesis 18%, raised cortisol 21% and lowered testosterone 24% [Lamon 2021].
  * Five nights at 4 h cut myofibrillar synthesis. Exercise (HIIT) during restriction **kept it at control levels** [Saner 2020].
  * The sleep–muscle hypothesis [Dattilo 2011] is a hypothesis paper.
* **Our reading:**
  * Exercise (HIIT, not lifting) protected protein synthesis during sleep restriction in Saner 2020. That suggests training under-slept is not harmful, but it does not show that lifting after a bad night beats resting.
  * Expect lower performance and higher RPE.
  * **Autoregulate rather than skip.** Keep the session but don't chase rep PRs, add about 1 RIR, or drop the last set of compounds. This lines up with "0 vs 1–2 RIR is equal for growth" [Refalo 2024b].
  * Train earlier in the day when possible [Craven 2022].
* Mitigations with some support [Knowles 2018]: caffeine, training with others, training soon after waking.
* "Reduce volume after bad sleep" is **coach practice**, extrapolated.

### 7.4 Home gyms and limited equipment

**Claim: Hypertrophy is achievable with light loads, bands, dumbbells and bodyweight if sets go close to failure.** **Moderate–strong** for the principle, **weak** for specific home-equipment trials.

* Loads of about 30–60% 1RM to failure match heavy loads for hypertrophy [Schoenfeld 2015, 2017b; Lopez 2021]. About 20% 1RM is too light [Lasevicius 2018]. Light loads must go near failure [Lasevicius 2022].
* Push-ups matched bench press for growth and strength at comparable loads [Kikuchi 2017; Calatayud 2015].
* Elastic bands matched conventional training for **strength** [Lopes 2019]. Band and bodyweight hypertrophy data are mostly from older adults [Ozaki 2020]. Supervision improved outcomes there.
* No dumbbell-only hypertrophy trial was found. ⚠
* **Implications:**
  * With limited load, the RIR problem gets worse: light sets mean high reps, and high-rep RIR estimates are poor (§2.3). Prefer **harder variations** to keep reps ≤20: deficit, single-leg, slower eccentric, paused. Or prescribe **to-failure / AMRAP sets** on safe movements rather than RIR targets.
  * Hardest gaps at home: **hamstring curls, lat pulldowns or rows, and leg extensions**. Use substitutes such as Nordic or slider curls, band pulldowns, inverted rows, and reverse Nordics or sissy squats. These substitutions are **coach practice**.

### 7.5 Novice vs trained

* Untrained lifters gain much faster and are less sensitive to program details [Ahtiainen 2003]. Most of the variable-manipulation research uses trained men in their 20s.
* Novices can use lower volume (about 8–12 sets/muscle/week) and simple linear or double progression. Advanced lifters need more volume and more careful fatigue management to keep progressing. This follows from the dose-response curve (§3.1) plus coach practice.
